I am an R&D engineer working at the intersection of machine learning and physics. Grounded in first principles, I build reliability from noisy sensor data through disciplined machine learning and algorithm design. 

Simply put, I love applied math. 

I originally found the joy of math through physics, and I came to physics via my long-time interest in music and philosophy. I think it was the physics of musical instruments (resonance & harmonics) and the power of audio signal processing that captured my imagination so much. Throw in the philosophical depth that the phrase "laws of nature" inspires, and I was hooked. I graduated from Tulane with a degree in physics and knew right out the gate that I wanted to go deeper. I wanted the Ph.D. The only question I had was whether to focus on physics or signal processing.

In the end, physics won out. After working for a year at a Tulane-affiliated polymerization monitoring company, I started the physics Ph.D. program at Boston University. Immediately, I joined the surface physics lab at BU where I operated an ultra high vacuum (UHV) chamber and ran helium atom scattering experiments to measure phonon properties at the surface of topological materials. To inform these experiments, I developed phenomenological models and used numerical methods to compute surface phonon properties. My research was going well and my work on topological phonons in CdTe was set to be my thesis. Then covid hit.

During covid, I began to have suspicions that the Ph.D. might not be the best path for me in the long run. I have great respect for those who complete a Ph.D., but it became increasingly clear to me that it must truly be a passion project --- and a grind of a passion project at that. Professionally, it seemed more advantageous to leave the program with a master's degree and start working. Indeed, I realized that my own satisfaction from work was not from physics, but from math, and there are many ways to do applied math without a Ph.D. 

So I left the Ph.D. and found a job working, as fate would have it, in signal processing. In that role, I worked with multi-axis magnetometry systems and developed algorithms to process the data they yield. My work was in many ways an exercise in observational science (as opposed to experimental science). That is just to highlight, when you take measurements in an environment that you cannot experimentally control, but only observe, there are all kinds of new data processing problems that you must address. For example, with the low frequency electromagnetic data I was working with, there are many sources of interference that must be mitigated to measure the very weak, low SNR signals you are interested in.

After several years working as a signal processing engineer, I left to work as a machine learning engineer in the nuclear space. Now I take data from radiation detectors (e.g. gamma spectrometers) and develop models that can provide fast, reliable information to an end user focusing on nuclear safety.
